Same area, different fence

Find rectangular gardens with the same area and compare their perimeters.

20 minIndependent readerTry independently

What we’re practising

Distinguish area from perimeter using factor pairs.

Before you begin

Imagine a garden of 24 square units with whole-number side lengths.

Let’s try it

  1. List the factor pairs of 24 to find possible rectangles.
  2. Draw or label each rectangle and confirm its area.
  3. Calculate each perimeter by adding all four sides.
  4. Choose the rectangle needing the least fence among these options.

Talk about it

Why can equal areas have unequal perimeters?

A little hint
  • The distinct pairs are 1×24, 2×12, 3×8 and 4×6.
Answer & what to notice

Their perimeters are 50, 28, 22 and 20 units; the 4 by 6 rectangle needs the least fence.

Area counts the inside squares; perimeter measures the outside boundary, which changes with shape.

Make it easier

Compare only 3 by 8 and 4 by 6.

Take it further

Repeat for area 36 and compare the most compact whole-number rectangle.

Away from the screen

Look for two arrangements of twelve paper squares with different perimeters.

What you’ll need

  • Squared paper or a drawn grid
  • Pencil

Use what you have

Draw labelled rectangles without exact grid squares.

Grown-up note

Use paper plans only; no fencing, digging or outdoor construction is involved.

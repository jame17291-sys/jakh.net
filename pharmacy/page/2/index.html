21What is the mechanism of action of penicillin-class antibiotics?

Answer

Penicillins inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis by blocking transpeptidase enzymes (penicillin-binding proteins), preventing cross-linking of peptidoglycan chains. This weakens the cell wall, causing the bacterium to burst (lyse).

22What is the 'first-pass effect' and which drugs are most affected by it?

Answer

When an orally administered drug is absorbed through the gut and passes through the portal vein to the liver before reaching systemic circulation, much of it is metabolized — reducing bioavailability. Highly metabolized drugs like morphine, nitroglycerin, and lidocaine show significant first-pass effects and may need higher oral doses or alternative routes.

23What are SSRIs and what are they primarily used to treat?

Answer

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors block reuptake of serotonin in synapses, increasing serotonin availability. They are the first-line treatment for depression and anxiety disorders (e.g. fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram).

24What is the WHO's ATC (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical) classification system?

Answer

A system for classifying drugs by the organ or body system they act on and their therapeutic, pharmacological, and chemical properties — organized in five levels. It is used globally for drug utilization research and comparison.

25What is heparin and how does it work as an anticoagulant?

Answer

Heparin is an injectable anticoagulant that activates antithrombin III, which then rapidly inactivates thrombin and factor Xa — key enzymes in the clotting cascade. Used to prevent and treat blood clots (DVT, pulmonary embolism).

26What is receptor downregulation and what clinical problem can it cause?

Answer

When a receptor is chronically activated (by an agonist drug), the cell reduces the number of available receptors. This causes drug tolerance — the same dose produces a weaker effect over time, requiring dose escalation. It is a major issue with opioids and beta-agonists in asthma.

27What is volume of distribution (Vd) and what does a high Vd indicate?

Answer

Vd is a theoretical volume relating the total amount of drug in the body to its plasma concentration (Vd = total drug / plasma concentration). A high Vd means the drug distributes extensively into tissues and is not confined to plasma — making it harder to remove by dialysis. Lipophilic drugs (e.g. chloroquine, amiodarone) have very high Vd values.

28What is the difference between bactericidal and bacteriostatic antibiotics and does it matter clinically?

Answer

Bactericidal antibiotics kill bacteria (e.g. penicillins, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides); bacteriostatic antibiotics inhibit growth, relying on immune clearance (e.g. tetracyclines, macrolides, sulfonamides). In immunocompromised patients or endocarditis, bactericidal drugs are preferred because the immune system cannot finish the job.

29What is Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) in drug safety?

Answer

A rare but life-threatening hypersensitivity reaction causing blistering and peeling of skin and mucous membranes. Commonly triggered by drugs including carbamazepine, allopurinol, lamotrigine, sulfonamides, and NSAIDs. It requires immediate discontinuation of the offending drug and intensive supportive care.

30What is the mechanism of action of metformin in type 2 diabetes?

Answer

Metformin primarily activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) in the liver, inhibiting gluconeogenesis (glucose production) and reducing hepatic glucose output. It also improves insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues. It does not cause hypoglycemia and is weight-neutral.

31What is the difference between Phase I and Phase II drug metabolism?

Answer

Phase I reactions (mainly by CYP450 enzymes in liver) introduce or unmask functional groups (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) — often activating prodrugs or making drugs more polar. Phase II reactions conjugate the drug or Phase I metabolite with endogenous molecules (glucuronidation, sulfation, acetylation) to make them more water-soluble for excretion.

32What is the mechanism of warfarin and why does it require careful monitoring?

Answer

Warfarin inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase, preventing regeneration of vitamin K — essential for producing clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X. Its effect is measured by INR. It has a narrow therapeutic index, is affected by diet (vitamin K foods) and many drug interactions (especially CYP2C9 inhibitors/inducers), requiring regular dose adjustment.

33What is the difference between tolerance, dependence, and addiction in the context of opioid drugs?

Answer

Tolerance: reduced drug effect with repeated use, requiring higher doses. Physical dependence: physiological adaptation where abrupt cessation causes withdrawal syndrome. Addiction: compulsive drug-seeking behavior despite harmful consequences — a neurological disease. All three can coexist; tolerance and dependence can occur without addiction.

34What is the difference between drug toxicity and drug allergy?

Answer

Drug toxicity is a dose-dependent, pharmacologically predictable harmful effect (e.g. acetaminophen overdose causing liver failure). Drug allergy is an immune-mediated reaction (hypersensitivity) that can occur at any dose, is unpredictable from pharmacology, and requires prior sensitization — ranging from mild rash to life-threatening anaphylaxis.

35What is the mechanism of aminoglycoside antibiotics and what is their main toxicity?

Answer

Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, amikacin) irreversibly bind the 30S ribosomal subunit, causing misreading of mRNA and production of faulty proteins — killing bacteria. Their main toxicities are nephrotoxicity (kidney damage) and ototoxicity (hearing/balance damage), which are dose-related and cumulative.

36What is the concept of 'therapeutic drug monitoring' (TDM) and which drug classes benefit most?

Answer

TDM is the practice of measuring drug concentrations in blood to optimize dosing. Most useful for drugs with a narrow therapeutic index and high inter-patient variability: aminoglycosides, vancomycin, lithium, phenytoin, digoxin, cyclosporine, and tacrolimus.

37What is pharmacogenomics and how can CYP2D6 polymorphisms affect drug therapy?

Answer

Pharmacogenomics studies how genetic variation affects drug response. CYP2D6 metabolizes ~25% of drugs (codeine, tamoxifen, many antidepressants). Poor metabolizers accumulate drug, risking toxicity; ultra-rapid metabolizers clear it too fast, losing efficacy. Codeine in ultra-rapid CYP2D6 metabolizers converts to dangerous morphine levels — an FDA-labeled risk.

38What is the difference between zero-order and first-order drug elimination kinetics?

Answer

First-order kinetics: a constant fraction of drug is eliminated per unit time (most drugs) — concentration-time curve is exponential. Zero-order kinetics: a constant amount is eliminated per unit time regardless of concentration (e.g. ethanol, phenytoin at high doses) — because eliminating enzymes are saturated. Zero-order kinetics causes disproportionate toxicity with dose escalation.

39What are the four phases of clinical drug trials?

Answer

Phase I: safety, tolerability, PK/PD in small healthy volunteers. Phase II: efficacy and safety in small patient populations, dose-finding. Phase III: large randomized controlled trials confirming efficacy and safety vs. placebo/standard care — required for regulatory approval. Phase IV: post-marketing surveillance for long-term or rare adverse effects.

40What is the concept of 'targeted therapy' in oncology and how do tyrosine kinase inhibitors work?

Answer

Targeted therapy attacks specific molecular abnormalities in cancer cells rather than killing all rapidly dividing cells. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) like imatinib (Gleevec) block overactive kinase enzymes (e.g. BCR-ABL in CML) that drive cancer cell proliferation. They are more selective and often have fewer side effects than traditional chemotherapy.

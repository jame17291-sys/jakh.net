21What is virtue ethics and how does it differ from Kantian ethics?

Answer

Virtue ethics (Aristotle) focuses on what kind of person one should be — asking 'what is a good person?' rather than 'what is the right action?' It emphasizes character traits (virtues like courage, honesty, justice) developed through habit. Kantian ethics focuses on duty and following universal moral rules regardless of character.

22What is Hume's problem of induction?

Answer

Hume argued that inductive reasoning (inferring general laws from observed cases — e.g. 'the sun has always risen, therefore it will rise tomorrow') cannot be logically justified. We have no rational basis to assume the future will resemble the past — we rely on habit and custom, not reason. This challenges the foundations of science and causation.

23What is the 'hard problem of consciousness' described by David Chalmers?

Answer

The hard problem asks why physical brain processes give rise to subjective, first-person experience (qualia) — the 'what it is like' to see red, feel pain, or hear music. The 'easy problems' (explaining cognitive functions) seem tractable in principle; the hard problem — why any physical process produces inner experience at all — may be fundamentally beyond materialist explanation.

24What is Peter Singer's argument for effective altruism?

Answer

Singer argues that if we can prevent something bad from happening without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do it — regardless of distance. This leads to the conclusion that wealthy people in developed countries have a strong moral obligation to give significantly to reduce extreme global poverty, since geographic distance has no moral relevance.

25What is the ship of Theseus paradox?

Answer

If every plank of a ship is gradually replaced, is it still the same ship? And if the original planks are reassembled elsewhere, which is the 'real' ship? The paradox questions the nature of identity and persistence through change — with implications for personal identity (if all our cells replace themselves, are we the same person?).

26What is Nietzsche's concept of the 'will to power'?

Answer

Nietzsche's concept that the fundamental drive underlying all human behavior is not pleasure-seeking (Schopenhauer) or survival, but the drive to express, exert, and expand one's power and creativity. It is not merely about domination — it includes artistic creation, intellectual mastery, and self-overcoming.

27What is Thomas Kuhn's concept of scientific paradigm shifts?

Answer

Kuhn argued in 'The Structure of Scientific Revolutions' that science does not progress by gradual accumulation but through revolutionary changes in 'paradigms' — the shared frameworks, assumptions, and methods of a scientific community. When anomalies accumulate, a crisis leads to a paradigm shift (e.g. Copernicus, Einstein, Darwin).

28What is the ontological argument for God's existence and who formulated it?

Answer

Formulated by Anselm of Canterbury (11th century): God is 'that than which nothing greater can be conceived.' If God existed only in the mind, something greater (one that also exists in reality) could be conceived — contradiction. Therefore God must exist in reality. Kant famously criticized it: 'existence is not a predicate.'

29What is Gödel's incompleteness theorem and why is it philosophically significant?

Answer

Kurt Gödel (1931) proved that in any consistent formal system powerful enough to express arithmetic, there are true statements that cannot be proved within that system. This showed that mathematics cannot be complete and self-verifying — demolishing Hilbert's program to ground all mathematics in a complete, consistent axiom system.

30What is the naturalistic fallacy in ethics, identified by G.E. Moore?

Answer

Moore argued that 'good' cannot be defined by any natural property (like pleasure, evolution, or God's will). Defining 'good' as something natural commits a fallacy because you can always meaningfully ask 'but is that thing actually good?' This is the 'open question argument'. It implies that ethical facts are distinct from natural facts.

31What is Ibn Sina's (Avicenna's) 'floating man' thought experiment?

Answer

Imagine a person created fully formed but floating in empty space, with no sensory input — eyes covered, not touching anything. Would they still be aware of their own existence? Ibn Sina argued yes — the person would be aware of themselves as a thinking subject, even with no sensory or bodily experience. This preceded Descartes' Cogito by centuries and establishes the soul's independent existence.

32What is Wittgenstein's private language argument?

Answer

The late Wittgenstein argued that a 'private language' — one that refers to inner experiences only the speaker can access — is impossible. Language requires public criteria for correct usage; without the possibility of public checking, there is no difference between 'using a word correctly' and 'thinking one is using it correctly.' This challenges Cartesian privacy of mind.

33What is Hannah Arendt's concept of the 'banality of evil'?

Answer

Based on her observation of Adolf Eichmann's trial (1961), Arendt argued that great evil is often committed not by monsters but by ordinary, thoughtless people who simply follow orders and bureaucratic routine without reflecting on the moral meaning of their actions. Evil requires no malicious intent — just the absence of thinking.

34What is the principle of sufficient reason (PSR)?

Answer

The philosophical principle, associated with Leibniz, that everything that exists has a sufficient reason or explanation for why it exists and is the way it is, rather than not existing or being otherwise. It underpins arguments for God's existence (cosmological argument) and is contested by philosophers who accept brute facts with no explanation.

35What is the paradox of the heap (sorites paradox)?

Answer

If you have a heap of sand and remove one grain, it's still a heap. Repeating this, when does it stop being a heap? The paradox arises because 'heap' is vague — there's no precise point at which the transition occurs. It challenges classical two-valued logic (true/false) and raises questions about vague predicates in language and law.

36What is the is-ought problem identified by David Hume?

Answer

Hume observed that many moral arguments make an unjustified jump from descriptive facts ('is') to normative conclusions ('ought'). From 'humans evolved to be social' you cannot derive 'humans ought to behave socially' without additional premises. This gap — the is-ought gap — is a foundational challenge in metaethics.

37What is Sartre's concept of 'existence precedes essence'?

Answer

Sartre argued that humans are not born with a predetermined nature or purpose (essence). We exist first, then create our own essence through choices and actions. This is radically liberating but also terrifying — we are 'condemned to be free' and fully responsible for who we become.

38What is the simulation hypothesis and what philosophical problems does it raise?

Answer

Nick Bostrom's trilemma: either civilizations go extinct before becoming technologically advanced enough to run simulations, or advanced civilizations don't run simulations, or we are almost certainly living in a simulation. It raises questions about the nature of reality (is it material?), identity, and whether any distinction exists between 'real' and 'simulated' experience.

39What is functionalism in philosophy of mind and what is the main objection against it?

Answer

Functionalism defines mental states by their causal roles — what inputs they respond to and what outputs they produce — rather than by their physical substrate. A mental state is 'pain' if it is caused by damage and causes avoidance behavior, regardless of whether it occurs in neurons or silicon. Main objection: John Searle's Chinese Room — a system can perform all the right functions without genuinely understanding anything (absent qualia).

40What is moral particularism and how does it differ from moral principlism?

Answer

Moral particularism (Jonathan Dancy) holds that moral features of a situation can vary in their moral relevance depending on context — a feature that counts in favor of an action in one situation may count against it in another. This contrasts with principlism (e.g. Kant, utilitarianism) which holds that moral principles apply universally across situations.

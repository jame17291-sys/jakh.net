21What is Pierre Bourdieu's concept of 'cultural capital'?

Answer

Non-financial social assets — knowledge, skills, education, and cultural tastes — that confer social advantage. It exists in three forms: embodied (dispositions), objectified (cultural goods), and institutionalised (educational credentials).

22What is the 'nature vs. nurture' debate in the context of human behaviour?

Answer

Whether human characteristics, abilities, and behaviours are primarily determined by genetics ('nature') or by environment, upbringing, and experience ('nurture'). Modern consensus: both interact — genes set potentials, environment shapes expression.

23What is the difference between monetary policy and fiscal policy?

Answer

Monetary policy is managed by central banks (interest rates, money supply) to control inflation and growth. Fiscal policy is managed by governments (taxation and spending) to influence the economy.

24What is 'soft power' in international relations, as defined by Joseph Nye?

Answer

The ability to shape the preferences of others through appeal and attraction rather than coercion or payment ('hard power'). It relies on culture, political values, and foreign policy legitimacy. E.g., the global appeal of American pop culture.

25What is 'labelling theory' in criminology and sociology?

Answer

The theory (associated with Howard Becker) that deviance is not inherent in an act but is constructed when society labels a person as deviant. Being labelled criminal can become a self-fulfilling prophecy, pushing individuals into further deviance.

26What is the concept of 'rites of passage' and give two examples from different cultures?

Answer

Rituals marking a transition from one social status to another (Arnold van Gennep). Three stages: separation, liminality (transition), and incorporation. Examples: bar mitzvah in Judaism (adulthood), Satere-Mawe bullet ant glove ritual in Brazil (warriorhood), marriage ceremonies, funerals.

27What is intersectionality and who developed the concept?

Answer

Developed by Kimberlé Crenshaw (1989), intersectionality is the framework for understanding how different aspects of a person's social and political identities (race, gender, class, sexuality, disability) combine to create overlapping systems of discrimination or privilege.

28What is the 'tragedy of the commons' and what solutions have been proposed?

Answer

Garrett Hardin's (1968) concept: when a shared resource (commons) is freely available to all, individual self-interest leads to overuse and depletion. Solutions: privatisation, government regulation, or Elinor Ostrom's third way — communities self-govern commons through negotiated rules (her 2009 Nobel Prize work).

29What is Huntington's 'Clash of Civilizations' thesis and what are its main criticisms?

Answer

Huntington (1993) argued that post-Cold War conflicts would be driven by cultural and religious differences between civilisational blocs rather than ideology. Critics argue it essentialises culture, ignores intra-civilisational conflicts, oversimplifies diversity within blocs, and can become a self-fulfilling prophecy that justifies conflict.

30What is Erving Goffman's 'dramaturgical model' of social interaction?

Answer

Goffman argued life is like a theatrical performance: in front-stage behaviour we manage impressions for audiences; in backstage behaviour we relax our performance. Key concepts: impression management, face-saving, stigma, and the presentation of self as a performed identity.

31What is 'market failure' and what are its main causes?

Answer

Market failure occurs when free markets fail to allocate resources efficiently. Main causes: externalities (spillover costs/benefits not priced, e.g., pollution), public goods (non-rival, non-excludable), information asymmetry, and monopoly power.

32What is Marcel Mauss's concept of 'the gift' and how does it challenge economic rationality?

Answer

In 'The Gift' (1925), Mauss argued that gift exchange in many societies is not voluntary or free — it creates binding obligations to receive, reciprocate, and give again (the 'spirit of the gift'). This challenges rational self-interest models by showing social bonds are built through obligated generosity, not market transactions.

33What is the 'democratic peace theory' and what is its main empirical finding?

Answer

The democratic peace theory holds that liberal democracies rarely (if ever) go to war with each other. The empirical finding is one of the most robust in international relations: dyads of mature democracies have an exceptionally low probability of militarised conflict.

34What is 'moral panic' (Stanley Cohen) and give a historical example?

Answer

A disproportionate, media-amplified social reaction to a group or behaviour deemed a threat to societal values. Cohen identified folk devils (the perceived threat), moral entrepreneurs (those stoking fear), and disproportionate response. Example: 1960s British media reaction to Mods and Rockers; or 1980s–90s 'Satanic panic' over heavy metal music.

35What is behavioural economics and how does it challenge classical economic theory?

Answer

Behavioural economics incorporates psychological insights to model real human decision-making. It challenges the 'rational economic man' assumption by demonstrating systematic biases: loss aversion (losses hurt more than equivalent gains), anchoring, present bias, and framing effects (Kahneman & Tversky).

36What is the 'out-of-Africa' theory of human origins and what is the main competing hypothesis?

Answer

Out-of-Africa (Recent African Origin) holds that all modern humans descend from a population that migrated from Africa roughly 60,000–70,000 years ago, replacing earlier archaic populations. The competing multi-regional hypothesis proposed parallel evolution in different regions. Genetic evidence overwhelmingly supports Out-of-Africa, though interbreeding with Neanderthals and Denisovans shows some regional gene flow.

37What is Antonio Gramsci's concept of 'hegemony'?

Answer

Gramsci used hegemony to describe how dominant classes maintain power not just through coercion but through ideological consent — shaping culture, education, and media so that subordinate groups accept the dominant worldview as 'common sense', making overt force unnecessary.

38What is Michel Foucault's concept of 'biopower'?

Answer

Biopower is Foucault's term for the way modern states regulate populations by controlling biological life — through medicine, public health, demographics, sexuality, and reproduction. It operates at two levels: disciplining individual bodies (anatomo-politics) and managing the population as a whole (biopolitics).

39What is the 'Lucas critique' in macroeconomics and why did it revolutionise economic modelling?

Answer

Robert Lucas (1976) argued that traditional macroeconomic models were invalid for policy evaluation because they assumed people's expectations remained fixed. When policy changes, rational agents update their behaviour, rendering historical statistical relationships unreliable. This forced economists to build 'microfounded' models with explicit optimising agents (DSGE models).

40What is 'structuration theory' (Anthony Giddens) and how does it resolve the agency-structure debate?

Answer

Giddens argued that structure and agency are mutually constitutive, not opposed — he called this the 'duality of structure'. Social structures (rules and resources) are both the medium of action and its outcome: agents draw on structures to act, and in doing so reproduce or transform those structures. Neither pure structuralism nor pure voluntarism is sufficient.

41What is 'principal-agent theory' and how does it explain problems of democratic governance?

Answer

Principal-agent theory analyses situations where one party (principal, e.g., voters) delegates work to another (agent, e.g., elected officials) but faces information asymmetry and misaligned incentives. In democratic governance, agents may act in self-interest rather than the principal's interest (moral hazard). Solutions: monitoring, transparency, electoral accountability, and institutional design.

42What is 'thick description' (Clifford Geertz) and why is it important in interpretive anthropology?

Answer

Geertz (1973) contrasted thin description (merely stating an observable fact — 'he winked') with thick description (explaining the cultural context that gives the act meaning — a conspiracy? a parody? a tic?). Interpretive anthropology treats culture as a text to be 'read' for layers of meaning, not just catalogued.

43What is 'world-systems theory' (Immanuel Wallerstein) and how does it critique modernisation theory?

Answer

Wallerstein argued that the capitalist world-economy is structured around core (wealthy, industrialised) and periphery (poor, raw-material exporting) nations linked by unequal exchange — with a semi-periphery in between. Contra modernisation theory, peripheral nations are not 'behind' on a developmental ladder; they are structurally underdeveloped by their role in the system.

44What is 'comparative advantage' (Ricardo) and how does it differ from absolute advantage?

Answer

Absolute advantage: producing more of a good using the same resources. Comparative advantage: producing a good at a lower opportunity cost — even if a country is worse at everything in absolute terms, it gains by specialising in what it does relatively best and trading. This underpins the economic case for free trade.

45What is 'path dependence' in political science and give a well-known example?

Answer

Path dependence means that early choices or contingent historical events constrain future options, making certain outcomes 'sticky' even when alternatives would be more efficient. Classic example: QWERTY keyboard layout persists despite potentially superior alternatives because switching costs are too high once a standard is locked in.

46What is 'post-colonialism' as a theoretical framework and who are its key thinkers?

Answer

Post-colonialism examines how colonial power shapes culture, identity, knowledge, and politics — and how its legacies persist after formal independence. Key thinkers: Frantz Fanon (The Wretched of the Earth — psychology of colonised peoples), Edward Said (Orientalism — Western discursive construction of 'the East'), Homi Bhabha (hybridity, ambivalence, mimicry), Gayatri Spivak ('Can the Subaltern Speak?').

47What is 'Keynesian economics' and how did it respond to the Great Depression?

Answer

John Maynard Keynes (1936) argued that aggregate demand — not supply — drives output and employment. During a depression, private demand collapses; the government should step in with deficit spending to stimulate demand. This justified New Deal-style interventions and contradicted classical 'Say's Law' that supply creates its own demand.

48What is 'structural functionalism' (Radcliffe-Brown, Parsons) and what are its main criticisms?

Answer

Structural functionalism views society as a system of interrelated parts (institutions) each fulfilling functions that maintain social stability. Criticisms: (1) it is teleological (explaining institutions by their effects, not causes); (2) it overstates consensus and ignores conflict; (3) it has a conservative bias, treating change as disruptive; (4) it cannot explain social change well (Merton's critique).

49What is 'deliberative democracy' and how does it differ from aggregative (majoritarian) democracy?

Answer

Aggregative democracy collects and tallies pre-formed preferences (voting). Deliberative democracy (Habermas, Rawls) holds that legitimate political decisions emerge from reasoned public debate among free and equal citizens, allowing preferences to be transformed through rational discourse — not just counted.

50What is 'risk society' (Ulrich Beck) and how does it describe modern social conditions?

Answer

Beck (1986) argued that industrial modernity replaced natural hazards with manufactured risks (nuclear accidents, environmental pollution, climate change) created by scientific-technical progress itself. In risk society, risks are global, invisible, not compensable, and cut across class lines — requiring new forms of reflexive governance and creating a 'sub-politics' beyond traditional institutions.

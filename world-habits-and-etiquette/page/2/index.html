21What is the significance of the number four in Japanese and Chinese cultures and why is it avoided?

Answer

The number four is considered unlucky because its pronunciation (shi in Japanese, sì in Chinese) sounds like the word for 'death'. This is called tetraphobia — hospitals and buildings often skip floor 4, and gifts are not given in sets of four.

22What is the concept of 'ubuntu' in southern African cultures?

Answer

Ubuntu (often translated as 'I am because we are') is a philosophy of human interconnectedness and community — the idea that a person's humanity is expressed through their relationships with others and their contribution to community well-being.

23What is the concept of 'face' (mianzi/lian) in Chinese culture and how does it affect business interactions?

Answer

Mianzi is social prestige/reputation; lian is moral character and integrity. Both must be protected. In business: never directly criticise, correct publicly, or refuse a request outright (which causes loss of face). Gifts, banquets, and indirect communication are used to preserve face. Causing someone to lose face is a serious, lasting offence.

24In Russia, what is the etiquette around vodka during a toast and what does refusing imply?

Answer

At formal gatherings, vodka is drunk as full shots during toasts — not sipped. The first toast is often to health (Za zdorovye). Refusing to drink during a toast, especially the first one, can be read as disrespect to the host or occasion. One must drain the glass in one go (do dna — 'to the bottom').

25What is the significance of the Obon festival in Japan and how is it different from the Western concept of Halloween?

Answer

Obon (mid-August) is a Buddhist festival honouring the spirits of deceased ancestors, who are believed to return to visit family. Families clean graves, light lanterns to guide spirits home, and perform Bon Odori dances. Unlike Halloween's fictional spooky imagery, Obon is a genuinely reverent, family-centred ancestral memorial.

26What is the Norwegian concept of 'Janteloven' and how does it shape social norms in Scandinavian societies?

Answer

Janteloven (coined by Aksel Sandemose) is a social code that discourages standing out, boasting, or considering yourself better than others. Its core: 'You are not to think you are anything special.' It promotes egalitarianism and collective modesty but can also suppress individual achievement and open ambition.

27Why is the 'thumbs up' gesture offensive in some Middle Eastern and West African countries?

Answer

In parts of Iran, Iraq, Nigeria, and other countries, the thumbs-up gesture is equivalent to raising the middle finger in Western cultures — a deeply offensive obscene insult. Western travellers who use it as a sign of approval can inadvertently cause serious offence.

28In which cuisine is it polite — and even expected — to make slurping sounds while eating noodles or soup?

Answer

Japan and China — slurping noodles (especially ramen in Japan) is a sign of enjoyment and appreciation for the food. It also aerates the noodles and food, enhancing flavour.

29What is 'gift-giving paralysis' in Japan and what are the cultural rules around giving and receiving gifts?

Answer

In Japan, gifts are a serious obligation requiring matching quality and occasion to status (omiyage culture). Rules: gifts are wrapped elaborately, presented and received with both hands and a bow, and generally not opened in front of the giver (to avoid visible disappointment). The obligation to reciprocate (お返し, okaeshi) makes gift exchanges complex and stressful.

30What is the significance of removing shoes before entering a home in many Asian and Middle Eastern cultures?

Answer

Shoes are considered bearers of outside dirt, impurity, and negative energy — physically and spiritually contaminating the home. In Muslim homes it also relates to ritual cleanliness (tahara). The home is a sacred space; removing shoes signals respect and a transition from the public to the private sphere.

31In many Arab cultures, when two men greet each other, what might the greeting involve beyond a handshake?

Answer

Cheek-to-cheek contact (nose touching in some Gulf cultures), extended handshakes while maintaining eye contact, and holding hands during conversation as a sign of warmth and brotherhood. The duration and physicality of the greeting signals closeness of the relationship.

32What is the concept of 'hygge' in Danish culture and why has it attracted global attention?

Answer

Hygge (roughly 'cosy well-being') describes a quality of cosiness and comfortable conviviality — candles, warm drinks, good company, and comfort over achievement. It has attracted attention as a contrast to hustle culture and a key part of Denmark's consistently high happiness rankings. It cannot be bought — it is an atmosphere intentionally created.

33In Morocco, what is the significance of serving mint tea three times, and what does each glass represent?

Answer

Moroccan mint tea (atay) is served in three glasses: the first is gentle as life, the second is strong as love, and the third is bitter as death. Refusing tea is considered impolite — accepting it is a sign of accepting the host's hospitality.

34In some West African cultures, what is 'libation pouring' and what purpose does it serve?

Answer

Libation is the ritual pouring of liquid (water, palm wine, alcohol) onto the ground to honour ancestors and spiritual forces. It opens ceremonies and gatherings by inviting ancestral spirits to attend and bless the event. The act maintains the link between the living and the dead.

35What is the meaning of the 'wai' greeting in Thailand and how does the height of the hands reflect social hierarchy?

Answer

The wai is performed by pressing palms together (prayer position) and bowing. The height of the hands and depth of the bow varies by status: greeting a monk requires hands at forehead level with a deep bow; greeting a peer is at chin level; juniors always initiate the wai first.

36What is the significance of the number eight in Chinese culture and how does it influence everyday decisions?

Answer

Eight (bā) sounds like the word for prosperity/fortune (fā). It is the luckiest number — people pay premium prices for phone numbers, license plates, and addresses with many eights. The 2008 Beijing Olympics opened at 8:08 PM on 8/8/08. Real estate prices for properties with '8' in the address are noticeably higher.

37What is the Ethiopian communal dining tradition of eating from a shared injera and how does it reflect cultural values?

Answer

Injera (spongy sourdough flatbread) is laid on a communal platter with stews (wot) on top. Everyone eats from the same bread without individual plates — using right-hand fingers to scoop. This practice (called 'gursha' when feeding others) embodies community, equality, and shared experience. Separate plates are considered antisocial.

38What is the Hindu concept of 'puja' and what role do offerings play in it?

Answer

Puja is a devotional ritual of worship performed to a deity, involving offerings of flowers, incense, light (aarti lamp), water, and food (prasad). The offerings are presented to the deity's image or statue; the deity's 'blessing' returns through prasad given back to worshippers. Puja can be performed at home shrines or temples.

39What is the concept of 'amae' (甘え) in Japanese psychology and how does it shape social expectations?

Answer

Amae (coined by psychologist Takeo Doi) describes a dependent presumption upon another's benevolence — a comfortable reliance on another's goodwill, presuming they will accommodate your needs without being asked. It underpins Japanese group harmony: members expect to be 'read' and accommodated rather than having to articulate every need explicitly.

40What is the Balinese concept of 'Tri Hita Karana' and how does it manifest in everyday life and ritual?

Answer

Tri Hita Karana ('three causes of well-being') is the Balinese Hindu philosophy of harmonious relationships between humans and God (Parahyangan), humans and other humans (Pawongan), and humans and nature (Palemahan). It manifests in daily offerings (canang sari), community temple worship, environmental conservation, and the integration of ritual into agricultural cycles.

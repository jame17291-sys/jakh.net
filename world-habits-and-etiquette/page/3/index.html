41What does anthropologist Edward Hall's concept of 'proxemics' reveal about cultural differences in personal space?

Answer

Proxemics (1966) categorises space into intimate (0–45 cm), personal (45–120 cm), social (1.2–3.6 m), and public zones. Cultures differ significantly: Arab, Latin American, and southern European cultures are 'contact cultures' comfortable with closer proximity; northern Europeans, Japanese, and North Americans are 'non-contact cultures' requiring more personal space.

42What anthropological theory explains why food taboos (like pork avoidance or beef avoidance) persist across cultures, and what are the main competing explanations?

Answer

Marvin Harris's cultural materialism: taboos are rational adaptations to ecological conditions (pigs are ecologically costly in the Middle East; cows are more valuable alive in South Asia). Mary Douglas's symbolic approach: taboos enforce categorical thinking — prohibited animals are 'anomalous' in their culture's classification system. A third view: taboos reinforce group boundaries and religious identity, regardless of practical benefit.

43What is 'wasta' in Arab culture and how does it function as a form of social capital?

Answer

Wasta is the use of personal connections and intermediaries to gain advantages in employment, bureaucracy, legal situations, or services. It operates through a network of obligations where helping someone creates a reciprocal debt. While parallel to Bourdieu's social capital, wasta is more formalised — embedded in extended family and tribal networks — and is debated for both facilitating social cohesion and undermining meritocracy.

44What is the Javanese concept of 'slametan' and how does it function as a communal ritual?

Answer

Slametan is a communal feast-prayer meal held to mark life transitions (births, deaths, marriages, moving house) and to invoke spiritual safety (slamet = safe/well). Neighbours and community gather, a religious leader recites prayers, and food is shared. Anthropologist Clifford Geertz analysed it as the core ritual of Javanese social and spiritual life in 'The Religion of Java' (1960).

45What is the concept of 'individualism vs. collectivism' in Hofstede's cultural dimensions and how does it predict etiquette differences?

Answer

Hofstede's IDV dimension measures how much individuals prioritise personal goals over group goals. High IDV (USA, Australia, UK): directness, speaking one's mind, individual achievement celebrated. Low IDV (Guatemala, Ecuador, Japan): group harmony prioritised, indirect communication, public criticism avoided, decisions made by consensus. This predicts: individualist cultures value punctuality for personal time; collectivist cultures prioritise relationship-building over schedules.

46What is 'kinesics' as developed by Ray Birdwhistell and how does it challenge the idea of universal body language?

Answer

Birdwhistell developed kinesics — the study of body movement as communication — and argued there are no universally understood gestures; all body language is culturally learned and contextually variable, like a language. This challenged Ekman's claim of universal facial expressions and emphasised that interpreting nonverbal communication requires cultural fluency, not just observation.

47What is 'emotional display rules' (Ekman & Friesen) and how do cultural norms regulate public emotional expression?

Answer

Display rules are cultural norms prescribing when, where, and to whom emotional expressions are appropriate. They cause the same underlying emotion to be expressed very differently: Japanese culture requires masking negative emotions in public (especially in professional settings); Italian culture encourages open expression; East Asian cultures value emotional restraint in formal contexts while West African cultures may express grief loudly and collectively.

48What is the concept of 'ritual pollution' in many world cultures and how does it shape social behaviour?

Answer

Ritual pollution (discussed by Mary Douglas in 'Purity and Danger') refers to states of impurity that require purification rituals before resuming social/religious participation — e.g., contact with the dead, menstruation, childbirth, or eating forbidden substances. These systems enforce social boundaries, signal category distinctions, and maintain group identity through purification practices.

49What is the cross-cultural difference between 'high-context' and 'low-context' communication cultures (Edward Hall) and how does it affect greetings and negotiation?

Answer

High-context cultures (Japan, China, Arab countries) rely heavily on implicit meaning, non-verbal cues, and shared context — much is unsaid but understood. Low-context cultures (USA, Germany, Scandinavia) communicate explicitly with words, expecting directness and written confirmation. In greetings, high-context cultures invest heavily in relationship-building before business; low-context cultures get to the point quickly.

50What is 'cultural lag' (William Ogburn) and how does it apply to changing etiquette norms in the digital age?

Answer

Cultural lag describes the time gap between when material culture (technology) changes and when non-material culture (norms, values, laws) adapts. In the digital age: phone etiquette at the table, the norms around social media use during conversations, and 'phubbing' (phone snubbing) represent areas where material technology advanced faster than social norms — causing friction between generations and cultures still negotiating appropriate behaviour.
